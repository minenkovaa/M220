    Section Module 5: Matrix Transformations

    Objectives

    • Interpret a matrix as a linear transformation from \(\mathbb{R}^n\) to \(\mathbb{R}^m\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Produce a matrix representation of a linear transformation given its action on the standard unit vectors.
      🔗
    • Identify basic geometric transformations of the plane.
      🔗
    • Interpret a matrix product as a composition of linear transformations.
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Linear Transformations

    Definition (Transformation). A transformation (function, mapping) \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) assigns to each \(x\in\mathbb{R}^n\) a vector \(T(x)\in\mathbb{R}^m\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Domain: \(\mathbb{R}^n\)   Codomain: \(\mathbb{R}^m\)   Range: \(\{T(x):x\in\mathbb{R}^n\}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Matrix Transformations. A transformation \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is a matrix transformation if there exists an \(m\times n\) matrix \(A\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(x)=Ax. \end{equation*}
    Dimension check. Vector \(x\) must have \(n\) entries and \(Ax\in\mathbb{R}^m\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Definition 46.

    Definition (Linear Transformation).\(T\)
    \begin{equation*} T(u+v)=T(u)+T(v), \qquad T(cu)=cT(u) \end{equation*}
    \(u,v\)\(c\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Theorem 47.

    Fact.\(A(u+v)=Au+Av, \qquad\text{ and } A(cu)=c(Au)\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Example: Computing Ax and Interpreting Ax = b

    Matrix and vectors. Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad u= \begin{bmatrix} 1.6\\ 0.9 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad v= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ -1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The matrix is singular and has rank 1.
    🔗
    Compute \(Au\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} Au = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1.6\\ 0.9 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2.5\\ 5 \end{bmatrix} = 2.5 \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Compute \(Av\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} Av = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1\\ 2 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ -1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Geometrically, all vectors in the direction of \(v\) are mapped to the zero vector.
    🔗
    Interpretation. Multiplication by \(A\) maps the entire plane onto the line generated by \(\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}\text{.}\) Solving \(Ax=b\) asks whether \(b\) lies on that line.
    🔗
    Questions.
    🔗
    • Where does \(A\) send \(0\text{?}\) Since the transformation is linear, \(T(0)=A0=0\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • What does it mean to solve \(Ax=b\text{?}\) It asks whether \(b\) lies in the column space of \(A\text{.}\)
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Connections to Calculus

    Linearity reminders (differentiation and integration). For differentiable functions \(f\) and \(g\text{,}\) and scalars \(a\) and \(b\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} af+bg \end{equation*}
    is linear in \(f\) and \(g\text{.}\) In particular, differentiation satisfies
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \frac{d}{dx}\bigl(af+bg\bigr) = af'+bg'. \end{equation*}
    Similarly, integration is a linear operation:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \int (af+bg)\,dx = a\int f\,dx + b\int g\,dx. \end{equation*}
    These properties are examples of linearity, the same idea that appears in matrix transformations.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Visual: Differentiation as a Matrix Transformation

    Explanation. We represent a polynomial by its coefficient vector. The derivative is a linear operator that can be written as matrix multiplication. The diagram below maps a coefficient vector to the coefficient vector of its derivative.
    🔗
    Example 1 (Quadratic, first derivative).
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p(x)=ax^2+bx+c \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p'(x)=2ax+b. \end{equation*}
    In terms of coefficient vectors,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} a\\ b\\ c \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} 2a\\ b \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a\\ b\\ c \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The matrix
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    represents differentiation on the space of quadratic polynomials.
    🔗
    Example 2 (Quadratic, second derivative).
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p''(x)=2a. \end{equation*}
    Using coefficient vectors,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} a\\ b\\ c \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} 2a \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a\\ b\\ c \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Thus the second-derivative operator is represented by the matrix
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Example 3 (Cubic, first derivative).
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} p(x)=ax^3+bx^2+cx+d \quad\Longrightarrow\quad p'(x)=3ax^2+2bx+c. \end{equation*}
    The corresponding coefficient transformation is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} a\\ b\\ c\\ d \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} 3a\\ 2b\\ c \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 2 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a\\ b\\ c\\ d \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the matrix
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 2 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    represents differentiation on the space of polynomials of degree at most three.
    🔗
    Connection. Differentiation is a linear transformation because
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} D(ap_1+bp_2) = aDp_1+bDp_2. \end{equation*}
    Consequently, differentiation can be represented by a matrix acting on coefficient vectors. This illustrates an important connection between calculus operators and matrix transformations on finite-dimensional
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Basic Geometric Transformations (Matrices from Notes)

    Shear.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3\\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    A shear transformation slides points horizontally while preserving vertical position. Straight lines remain straight, but angles and shapes may be distorted.
    🔗
    Contraction.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} \frac12 & 0\\ 0 & \frac12 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    A contraction scales all vectors toward the origin by a factor of \(\frac12\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Dilation.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0\\ 0 & 2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    A dilation scales all vectors away from the origin by a factor of \(2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Rotation (90°).
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} 0 & -1\\ 1 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    This matrix rotates every vector counterclockwise by \(90^\circ\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection The Matrix of a Linear Transformation

    Subsubsection Objectives

    • Find the standard matrix corresponding to a given linear transformation.
      🔗
    • Understand onto and one-to-one and recognize them from the matrix.
      🔗
    • Analyze a linear transformation by its standard matrix.
      🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Standard Vectors (Standard Basis)

    Definition. The standard vectors (or standard basis vectors) in \(\mathbb{R}^n\) are
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} e_1= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0\\ \vdots\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad e_2= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1\\ \vdots\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad \dots, \qquad e_n= \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 0\\ \vdots\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Each vector \(e_j\) has a \(1\) in the \(j\)-th position and \(0\) everywhere else.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Coordinate Decomposition

    For a vector
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x= \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ x_2\\ \vdots\\ x_n \end{bmatrix} \in\mathbb{R}^n, \end{equation*}
    we may write
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x = x_1e_1+x_2e_2+\cdots+x_ne_n, \end{equation*}
    where \(e_1,\dots,e_n\) are the standard basis vectors.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsubsection Standard Matrix

    Standard matrix (columns are images of the standard basis vectors). If \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is a linear transformation, then there is a unique matrix \(A\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(x)=Ax. \end{equation*}
    The columns of \(A\) are the images of the standard basis vectors:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A = \bigl[ T(e_1)\; T(e_2)\; \dots\; T(e_n) \bigr]. \end{equation*}
    Thus, to determine the standard matrix of a linear transformation, compute the images of the standard basis vectors and place those image vectors into the columns of a matrix.
    🔗
    Remark. We already know that every matrix transformation is linear. Combining this fact with the theorem above shows that the set of all linear transformations from \(\mathbb{R}^n\) to \(\mathbb{R}^m\) is equivalent to the set of all \(m\times n\) matrices.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 48. Finding a Standard Matrix.

    Find the standard matrix for
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} 2x_1+x_2\\ 3x_2 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Compute the images of the standard basis vectors:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(e_1) = T \left( \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} \right) = \begin{bmatrix} 2\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} T(e_2) = T \left( \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right) = \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A = \begin{bmatrix} T(e_1) & T(e_2) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1\\ 0 & 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsubsection Example (Rotation by \(\pi/5\))

    A counterclockwise rotation by \(\theta=\pi/5\) in \(\mathbb{R}^2\) has standard matrix
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R_\theta = \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta & -\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta & \cos\theta \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(\theta=\pi/5\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} R_{\pi/5} = \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\pi/5) & -\sin(\pi/5)\\ \sin(\pi/5) & \cos(\pi/5) \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsubsection Onto and One-to-One

    • Onto. A transformation is onto if every \(b\in\mathbb{R}^m\) is equal to \(T(x)\) for some \(x\in\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • One-to-one. A transformation is one-to-one if \(T(x)=T(y)\) implies \(x=y\text{.}\)
      🔗
    Matrix criteria. If \(A\) is the standard matrix of \(T\text{,}\) then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T\text{ is onto} \iff \text{columns of }A\text{ span }\mathbb{R}^m \iff A\text{ has a pivot in every row}. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} T\text{ is one-to-one} \iff \text{columns of }A\text{ are linearly independent} \iff A\text{ has a pivot in every column}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Subsubsection Examples

    • Consider \(T:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\text{,}\) defined by \(x\mapsto 2x\text{.}\) Its standard matrix is
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} [2]. \end{equation*}
      Since the matrix has a pivot in its only row and column, the transformation is both onto and one-to-one.
      🔗
      🔗
    • Consider
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} T:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      Its standard matrix is
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
      This transformation is not onto because its range is only the [cross-reference to target(s) "x-axis" missing or not unique] in the plane. It is also not one-to-one because the second column is the zero vector.
      🔗
      🔗

    Subsection Onto and One-to-One

    Onto. A linear transformation \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) is called onto if every vector in the codomain \(\mathbb{R}^m\) is the image of some vector in the domain. That is, for every \(b\in\mathbb{R}^m\text{,}\) there exists an \(x\in\mathbb{R}^n\) such that
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(x)=b. \end{equation*}
    One-to-One. A linear transformation is called one-to-one if different vectors in the domain are always mapped to different vectors in the codomain. Equivalently,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(x)=T(y) \quad \Longrightarrow \quad x=y. \end{equation*}
    For matrix transformations, onto and one-to-one can be determined by examining the pivots of the standard matrix.
    🔗
    Matrix Criteria. Let \(A\) be the standard matrix of a linear transformation \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T \text{ is onto} \iff \text{the columns of }A\text{ span }\mathbb{R}^m \iff A\text{ has a pivot in every row}. \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} T \text{ is one-to-one} \iff \text{the columns of }A\text{ are linearly independent} \iff A\text{ has a pivot in every column}. \end{equation*}

    Example 49. A Transformation that is Onto and One-to-One.

    Consider the transformation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}, \qquad T(x)=2x. \end{equation*}
    Its standard matrix is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A=[2]. \end{equation*}
    The matrix has a pivot in its only row and its only column. Therefore, the transformation is both onto and one-to-one.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 50. A Transformation that is Neither Onto nor One-to-One.

    Consider the transformation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T:\mathbb{R}^2\to\mathbb{R}^2, \qquad \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ x_2 \end{bmatrix} \mapsto \begin{bmatrix} x_1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Its standard matrix is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0\\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    The second row contains no pivot, so the columns do not span \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{.}\) Therefore, the transformation is not onto.
    🔗
    The second column is the zero vector, so the columns are not linearly independent. Therefore, the transformation is not one-to-one.
    🔗
    Geometrically, every vector in the plane is projected onto the \(x\)-axis. The range is the set
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} x\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} : x\in\mathbb{R} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    Since the range is only the \(x\)-axis rather than all of \(\mathbb{R}^2\text{,}\) the transformation is not onto. Many different vectors have the same image, so it is not one-to-one.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Composition of Linear Transformations

    Suppose \(T:\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^m\) and \(S:\mathbb{R}^m\to\mathbb{R}^p\) are linear transformations with standard matrices \(A\) and \(B\text{,}\) respectively.
    🔗
    If
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} T(x)=Ax \qquad\text{and}\qquad S(y)=By, \end{equation*}
    then the composition of the transformations is obtained by applying \(T\) first and then \(S\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (S\circ T)(x) = S(T(x)). \end{equation*}
    Substituting \(T(x)=Ax\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (S\circ T)(x) = S(Ax) = B(Ax). \end{equation*}
    Using associativity of matrix multiplication,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (S\circ T)(x) = (BA)x. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, the standard matrix of the composition \(S\circ T\) is the product \(BA\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The diagram illustrates the path
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x \xrightarrow{\;T\;} y=T(x) \xrightarrow{\;S\;} z=S(y). \end{equation*}
    Applying the two transformations in succession is equivalent to applying a single transformation whose matrix is the product \(BA\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Thus,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} S\circ T \quad\longleftrightarrow\quad BA. \end{equation*}
    This provides a geometric interpretation of matrix multiplication: multiplying matrices corresponds to composing linear transformations.
    🔗
