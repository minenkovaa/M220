    Section Module 9: Orthogonality

    Objectives

    • O1 Determine whether a given map is an inner product.
      🔗
    • O2 Determine whether two vectors from an abstract vector space are orthogonal.
      🔗
    • O3 Use the transpose to find a basis for the orthogonal complement of a vector space.
      🔗
    • O4 Perform orthogonal decomposition.
      🔗
    • O5 Use Gram-Schmidt orthogonalization to find an orthogonal basis.
      🔗
    • O6 Find an orthonormal basis from an orthogonal basis.
      🔗
    🔗
    For more details on this material, see Linear Algebra: An Interactive Introduction (Orthogonality Chapter).
    🔗

    Subsection Inner Products

    How do we apply the concepts of length, distance, and orthogonality to an abstract vector space setting?
    🔗

    Definition 119. Inner Product.

    Let \(u,v,w\in V\) be vectors in a vector space \(V\text{,}\) and let \(c\) be a scalar.
    🔗
    An inner product is a map
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \langle \cdot,\cdot\rangle : V\times V \to \mathbb{R} \end{equation*}
    (or \(\mathbb{C}\)) that takes two vectors from \(V\) and returns a scalar, satisfying:
    🔗
    • Symmetry
      \begin{equation*} \langle u,v\rangle=\langle v,u\rangle \end{equation*}
      or, in the complex case,
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \langle u,v\rangle = \overline{\langle v,u\rangle}. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    • Linearity in the first argument
      \begin{equation*} \langle u+v,w\rangle = \langle u,w\rangle+\langle v,w\rangle \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} \langle cu,v\rangle = c\langle u,v\rangle. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    • Positive Definiteness
      \begin{equation*} \langle u,u\rangle>0 \qquad \text{whenever }u\neq 0. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    🔗

    Aside: Study Tip.

    Inner product generalizes the dot product. Everything about lengths, angles, and orthogonality comes from it.
    🔗

    Remark 120.

    For any vector \(u\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \langle 0,u\rangle=0. \end{equation*}
    Also,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \langle u,u\rangle=0 \Longrightarrow u=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Remark 121. Standard Inner Product on \(\mathbb{R}^n\).

    If \(V=\mathbb{R}^n\text{,}\) then the most commonly used inner product is the dot product:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \langle u,v\rangle = u\cdot v = u^\top v = u_1v_1+u_2v_2+\cdots+u_nv_n. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Theorem 122. The Dot Product is an Inner Product.

    The dot product defines an inner product on \(\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Proof.

    We verify the defining properties.
    🔗
    1. Symmetry:
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \langle u,v\rangle = u_1v_1+\cdots+u_nv_n = v_1u_1+\cdots+v_nu_n = \langle v,u\rangle. \end{equation*}
      Therefore the dot product is symmetric.
      🔗
      🔗
    2. Additivity:
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \langle u+v,w\rangle = (u_1+v_1)w_1+\cdots+(u_n+v_n)w_n \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} = (u_1w_1+\cdots+u_nw_n) + (v_1w_1+\cdots+v_nw_n) \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} = \langle u,w\rangle + \langle v,w\rangle. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    3. Homogeneity:
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \langle cu,v\rangle = (cu_1)v_1+\cdots+(cu_n)v_n \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} = c(u_1v_1+\cdots+u_nv_n) \end{equation*}
      \begin{equation*} = c\langle u,v\rangle. \end{equation*}
      Thus the dot product is linear in its first argument.
      🔗
      🔗
    4. Positive definiteness:
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} \langle u,u\rangle = u_1^2+u_2^2+\cdots+u_n^2. \end{equation*}
      If \(u\neq 0\text{,}\) then at least one component of \(u\) is nonzero, so
      🔗
      \begin{equation*} u_1^2+\cdots+u_n^2>0. \end{equation*}
      Hence the dot product is positive definite.
      🔗
      🔗
    Therefore the dot product is an inner product on \(\mathbb{R}^n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u\cdot v=\|u\|\|v\|\cos\theta. \end{equation*}

    Aside: Study Tip.

    Dot product measures alignment.
    • Positive ⇒ acute angle.
      🔗
    • Zero ⇒ perpendicular.
      🔗
    • Negative ⇒ obtuse angle.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection The Length of a Vector

    Definition 123. Length (Norm).

    The length (or norm) of a vector \(v\) is the nonnegative scalar
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|v\| = \sqrt{v\cdot v} = \sqrt{v_1^2+\cdots+v_n^2}. \end{equation*}
    Equivalently,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|v\|^2=v\cdot v, \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \|v\| = \sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|v\| = \sqrt{4^2+3^2} = 5. \end{equation*}

    Aside: Study Tip.

    Norm equals the distance from the origin to the tip of the vector. This is exactly the same idea as the length of a vector in geometry.
    🔗

    Remark 124.

    For any scalar \(c\text{,}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|cv\| = |c|\,\|v\|. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Definition 125. Unit Vector.

    A vector whose length is \(1\) is called a unit vector.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 126.

    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2\\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad v= \begin{bmatrix} \frac12\\ 0\\ \frac{\sqrt3}{2} \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Find \(\|u\|\text{,}\) \(\|v\|\text{,}\) and \(u\cdot v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First compute the norm of \(u\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|u\| = \sqrt{1^2+2^2+3^2} = \sqrt{14}. \end{equation*}
    Next compute the norm of \(v\text{:}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|v\| = \sqrt{\frac14+0+\frac34} = 1. \end{equation*}
    Finally compute the dot product:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u\cdot v = 1\left(\frac12\right) + 2(0) + 3\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right) = \frac12+\frac{3\sqrt3}{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Distance in \(\mathbb{R}^n\)

    Definition 127. Distance.

    For vectors \(u,v\in\mathbb{R}^n\text{,}\) the distance between \(u\) and \(v\) is defined by
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{dist}(u,v) = \|u-v\|. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{dist}(u,v) = \|u-v\|. \end{equation*}

    Aside: Study Tip.

    The distance between two points is the length of the vector connecting them.
    🔗

    Example 128.

    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2\\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad v= \begin{bmatrix} -3\\ 2\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Find \(\operatorname{dist}(u,v)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    First compute \(u-v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u-v = \begin{bmatrix} 1-(-3)\\ 2-2\\ 3-0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4\\ 0\\ 3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Now compute its norm:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \operatorname{dist}(u,v) = \|u-v\| = \sqrt{4^2+0^2+3^2} = 5. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside: Problem-Solving Strategy.

    Step 1: Find \(u-v\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Step 2: Compute the norm \(\|u-v\|\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Angles in \(\mathbb{R}^n\)

    For nonzero vectors \(u\) and \(v\text{,}\) the dot product is related to the angle \(\theta\) between them by the formula
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u\cdot v = \|u\|\,\|v\|\cos\theta. \end{equation*}

    Aside: Interpretation.

    The angle \(\theta\) measures how closely the vectors point in the same direction.
    🔗
    Solving for \(\theta\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \cos\theta = \frac{u\cdot v} {\|u\|\,\|v\|}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \theta = \arccos \left( \frac{u\cdot v} {\|u\|\,\|v\|} \right). \end{equation*}

    Aside: Geometric Meaning of the Dot Product.

    Although angles become less geometric in higher dimensions, one particularly important case is
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \theta=\frac{\pi}{2}. \end{equation*}
    This corresponds to vectors that are perpendicular, or orthogonal.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Orthogonal Vectors

    Definition 130. Orthogonal Vectors.

    Two vectors \(u\) and \(v\) are called orthogonal if
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u\cdot v=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Example 131.

    Determine whether
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u= \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 2\\ 3 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad v= \begin{bmatrix} -3\\ 0\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \end{equation*}
    are orthogonal.
    🔗
    Solution.
    \begin{equation*} u\cdot v = 1(-3)+2(0)+3(1) = 0. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, \(u\) and \(v\) are orthogonal.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside: Study Tip.

    Orthogonal means perpendicular. Neither vector contributes any component in the direction of the other.
    🔗

    Remark 132.

    Two vectors are orthogonal if and only if
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u\cdot v=0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} u\cdot v=0. \end{equation*}

    Aside: Study Tip.

    Orthogonal means perpendicular. Neither vector contributes any component in the direction of the other.
    🔗

    Theorem 133. Pythagorean Theorem.

    Two vectors \(u\) and \(v\) are orthogonal if and only if
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|u+v\|^2 = \|u\|^2+\|v\|^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Aside: Study Tip.

    This theorem is actually equivalent to orthogonality. If the equation holds, the vectors must be perpendicular to each other.
    🔗

    Proof.

    Suppose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \langle u,v\rangle=0. \end{equation*}
    Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|u+v\|^2 = \langle u+v,u+v\rangle. \end{equation*}
    Expanding the inner product gives
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} = \langle u,u\rangle + \langle u,v\rangle + \langle v,u\rangle + \langle v,v\rangle. \end{equation*}
    Since the vectors are orthogonal,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \langle u,v\rangle = \langle v,u\rangle = 0. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|u+v\|^2 = \|u\|^2+\|v\|^2. \end{equation*}
    Conversely, suppose
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|u+v\|^2 = \|u\|^2+\|v\|^2. \end{equation*}
    Expanding the left-hand side,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \|u+v\|^2 = \|u\|^2 + 2\langle u,v\rangle + \|v\|^2. \end{equation*}
    Comparing the two expressions yields
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 2\langle u,v\rangle=0. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \langle u,v\rangle=0. \end{equation*}
    Therefore \(u\) and \(v\) are orthogonal.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Orthogonal Complements

    Definition 134. Orthogonal Complement.

    Let \(W\) be a subset of a vector space. If a vector \(z\) is orthogonal to every vector in \(W\text{,}\) then we say that \(z\) is orthogonal to \(W\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The set of all vectors orthogonal to \(W\) is called the orthogonal complement of \(W\) and is denoted \(W^\perp\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside: Study Tip.

    The orthogonal complement contains every vector that is perpendicular to the entire subspace, not just to a single vector. In most situations, W⊥ is itself a subspace.
    🔗

    Remark 135.

    A vector belongs to the orthogonal complement exactly when it is orthogonal to every vector in the span of the set:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\in W^\perp \iff x \text{ is orthogonal to } \operatorname{span}(W). \end{equation*}
    Furthermore, \(W^\perp\) is a subspace.
    🔗
    🔗

    Theorem 136. Orthogonal Complement Theorem.

    Let \(A\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (\operatorname{Row}A)^\perp = \operatorname{Nul}(A), \end{equation*}
    and
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} (\operatorname{Col}A)^\perp = \operatorname{Nul}(A^\top ). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Proof.

    Let \(x\in\operatorname{Nul}(A)\text{.}\) Then
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} Ax=0. \end{equation*}
    Since each entry of \(Ax\) is the dot product of a row of \(A\) with \(x\text{,}\) every row of \(A\) is orthogonal to \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x\in(\operatorname{Row}A)^\perp. \end{equation*}
    The reverse inclusion is established similarly.
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 137.

    Let
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} W = \operatorname{span} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 1\\ 0\\ 3 \end{bmatrix} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    Find \(W^\perp\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Solution.
    Place the generator of \(W\) into a row matrix:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} A= \begin{bmatrix} 1 &0 &3 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    By the Orthogonal Complement Theorem,
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} W^\perp = \operatorname{Nul}(A). \end{equation*}
    Solve the equation
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x+3z=0. \end{equation*}
    Hence
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} x=-3z. \end{equation*}
    Let \(y=s\) and \(z=t\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Then every solution has the form
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{bmatrix} x\\ y\\ z \end{bmatrix} = s \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + t \begin{bmatrix} -3\\ 0\\ 1 \end{bmatrix}. \end{equation*}
    Therefore
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} W^\perp = \operatorname{span} \left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 0\\ 1\\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} -3\\ 0\\ 1 \end{bmatrix} \right\}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Aside: Procedure for Finding an Orthogonal Complement.

    1. Put the generators of \(W\) into a matrix as rows.
      🔗
    2. Find the null space of that matrix.
      🔗
    3. Express the solution set as a span.
      🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection Summary

    An inner product generalizes the dot product and provides a way to measure geometric properties in a vector space.
    🔗
    • An inner product satisfies symmetry, linearity, and positive definiteness.
      🔗
    • The norm (length) of a vector is \(\|v\|=\sqrt{\langle v,v\rangle}\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • The distance between vectors is \(\operatorname{dist}(u,v)=\|u-v\|\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • The angle between nonzero vectors is determined by
      \begin{equation*} u\cdot v = \|u\|\|v\|\cos\theta. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    • Two vectors are orthogonal if \(u\cdot v=0\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • Orthogonal vectors satisfy the Pythagorean Theorem:
      \begin{equation*} \|u+v\|^2 = \|u\|^2+\|v\|^2. \end{equation*}
      🔗
    • The orthogonal complement \(W^\perp\) consists of all vectors orthogonal to every vector in \(W\text{.}\)
      🔗
    • For a matrix \(A\text{,}\)
      \begin{equation*} (\operatorname{Row}A)^\perp = \operatorname{Nul}(A), \qquad (\operatorname{Col}A)^\perp = \operatorname{Nul}(A^\top ). \end{equation*}
      🔗

    Aside: Big Picture.

    \begin{equation*} \small\text{Inner Product} \Longrightarrow \text{Angles} \Longrightarrow \text{Orthogonality} \Longrightarrow \text{Pythagorean Theorem} \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \small\text{Orthogonality} \Longrightarrow \text{Orthogonal Complements }(W^\perp) \end{equation*}
    The central idea of the chapter is that the inner product is the tool that creates geometry in vector spaces, allowing us to measure lengths, distances, angles, and perpendicularity just as we do in ordinary Euclidean space.
